What net terms actually look like, by industry

Net 30 is the nominal default almost everywhere, but the term a small supplier actually signs is set by the buyer's size and category, not the supplier's preference. Net 60 and Net 90 are structural in categories with long procurement or production cycles.

IndustryTypical contracted termsTypical realized DSOWhy
Manufacturing (parts/components to OEMs) NET 60 NET 90 45–60 days Long procurement cycles; buyer volume dictates terms — e.g. an auto-parts supplier agreeing to Net 90 because of the OEM's order size.
Professional services NET 30 NET 45 45–65 days Milestone/retainer billing plus internal approval chains push realized collection well past the stated term.
Wholesale & distribution NET 30 NET 60 30–50 days Invoices ride on shipped, delivery-documented goods, which narrows the dispute window and speeds collection.
Construction / subcontracting NET 60+ 90–120 days Progress billing tied to milestones, plus 5–10% retainage held until project completion.
Retail-facing B2B NET 30 ~15–30 days Faster cash cycles and standardized EDI/AP processes.
  • Enterprise and government buyers "typically require Net-30 to Net-90 terms," and large buyers with strong balance sheets push for Net 60/90 as a matter of course rather than exception. Source: Now Corp, Resolve Pay
  • A small supplier offering the buyer's requested Net 30–90 is effectively extending an interest-free loan for that period — the working-capital strain is structural, not incidental. Source: JPMorgan Commercial Banking

Days Sales Outstanding, 2025–2026

DSO measures the gap between the contracted term and what actually gets collected — the number that matters for a supplier's real cash position.

  • By company size: small businesses target 15–30 days DSO, mid-market runs 30–45 days, enterprise 45–60+ days — the asymmetry means a small supplier waits nearly as long as an enterprise buyer's whole DSO just to get paid by one. Source: Stuut.ai
  • The broad US median DSO was 40.50 days in Q4 2025 and 40.12 days in Q1 2026, per Credit Research Foundation trade-receivables surveys. Source: CreditPulse (citing CRF)
  • One bright spot: US small businesses were paid an average of 7.8 days late in Q4 2025 — the shortest lag in four years, suggesting slow, partial improvement. Source: Stuut.ai
  • 59% of small businesses had overdue invoices in 2026 (up from 56% in 2025), averaging $17.7K unpaid at any given time. Source: Intuit QuickBooks 2026 report
  • Knock-on effects of late payment: 26% of owners delayed their own salary, 19% took on debt or used credit cards they otherwise wouldn't have, 18% paid a bill late and ate a penalty, and 49% describe standard processing delays as a critical or moderate cash-flow gap. Source: Intuit QuickBooks 2025 report
  • The Fed's Small Business Credit Survey (fielded Sep–Nov 2025, 6,500+ firms) tracks trade credit and factoring usage alongside loans and lines of credit as financing small firms turn to when cash is tight. Source: Fed Small Business, Fed Communities summary

Dynamic discounting — the mechanics

C2FO and SAP Taulia run the two dominant dynamic-discounting marketplaces. Mechanically identical idea: the buyer pays early using its own cash, in exchange for a discount that scales with how many days early it pays. No bank, factor, or third-party lender sits in the transaction.

How the offer is priced

  • The discount is expressed and calculated as an APR: discount % = APR × (days paid early ÷ 365). The earlier the payment, the bigger the discount. Source: C2FO glossary
  • Reverse the formula to check if it's a good deal: annualized rate = discount% ÷ (100% − discount%) × 365 ÷ days early. A 2% discount for 20 days early works out to ~37% annualized — expensive. Correction: this rate depends only on the discount and the number of days paid early, not on whether the underlying term is Net 30 or Net 60 — the total contract length doesn't change the math. To bring that same 2% discount down to a more reasonable ~12% annualized, you'd need to be paid roughly 60 days early, not 20. Formula per Beancount.io supplier guide; corrected from an earlier draft of this page that incorrectly implied the Net 60 vs. Net 30 baseline changed the annualized rate.
  • C2FO reports an average buyer-side APR around 5.82%, achieved by blending offers across suppliers (e.g. combining a 6% APR supplier with a 4% APR supplier to hit a 5% target). Source: PYMNTS. (An earlier draft compared this to ~65% online-lender APRs — removed as a headline stat since the two aren't a like-for-like comparison: different products, different risk being priced.)
  • SAP Taulia's version works the same way but is embedded directly in SAP AP workflows; in 2025, Applied Materials saw a 23% jump in supplier usage, which the vendor reads as suppliers under more cash-flow pressure. Source: ERP Today, SAP Taulia

Dynamic discounting

C2FO · SAP Taulia · buyer-funded, no lender

Pros

  • Cheapest early-payment option when it's available — no credit-risk premium since the buyer already knows and trusts the invoice
  • Cash in 24–48 hours once accepted
  • Supplier keeps full invoice ownership; no factor takes over the customer relationship
  • Rate is transparent and supplier-selected, not dictated

Cons

  • Only exists if the buyer has opted in to a program — supplier can't force it
  • Effective APR can be brutal on already-short terms (Net 30) even though it looks like "just 2%"
  • Recurring use quietly erodes margin if not tracked as a real financing cost
  • Buyer, not supplier, controls how much capital is made available

Factoring vs. dynamic discounting

Both get a supplier cash before the due date. The economic difference is who absorbs the buyer's credit risk — and that's exactly what shows up in the price.

Dynamic discountingInvoice factoring
Who funds itThe buyer, with its own cashA third-party factor / lender
Who holds the credit riskNobody new — buyer already owed the moneyThe factor (this is what it's charging for)
Typical cost0.5%–2% of invoice value1%–5% of invoice value per month
Advance rate100% minus discount, on acceptance~80–90% upfront (transportation niches: 97–100%+)
Invoice ownershipStays with supplierSold to the factor
Extra feesGenerally none beyond the discountOften stacks application, service, sign-up and wire fees on top
Needs buyer opt-in?Yes — buyer must run a programNo — supplier can factor unilaterally

Sources: Resolve Pay, FundThrough, eCapital, SMB Compass

Invoice factoring

FundThrough · eCapital · third-party funded

Pros

  • Available regardless of whether the buyer participates in anything
  • Removes the receivable — and its collection risk — from the supplier's books entirely
  • Some niches (e.g. freight/transportation) get very high advance rates, 97–100%+
  • Faster to arrange than negotiating a program with every buyer

Cons

  • 3–10x more expensive than dynamic discounting on an annualized basis
  • Fees pile up: invoice-aging tiers, application/service/wire fees on top of the base rate
  • A third party now contacts your customer for collection — can strain the buyer relationship
  • Invoices over ~60 days old are priced much higher or declined outright

Negotiation leverage & prompt-payment advocacy

Peer-reviewed corporate-finance research is blunt about who has the power in these relationships — and US advocacy has responded with voluntary pledges rather than legal mandates.

What the finance literature finds

  • "Suppliers with weak bargaining power towards their customers are more likely to extend trade credit, have a larger share of goods sold on credit, and offer a longer payment period before imposing penalties." Fabbri & Klapper, "Bargaining Power and Trade Credit," J. Corporate Finance
  • A one-standard-deviation increase in a listed firm's bargaining power as a buyer increases its use of payables financing by 1.73% of sales — the squeeze is measurable, not anecdotal. Source: RePEc / ScienceDirect
  • Better access to bank credit measurably improves a supplier's bargaining position specifically against powerful (not weak) customers — meaning financing tools double as negotiating leverage, not just a workaround. Source: "Bargaining power and trade credit: heterogeneous effect of credit contractions"
  • In a study of major public retail buyers (40 large retailers, including big-box-scale companies) and their suppliers, when the buyer slows payment by one month, a financially constrained supplier cuts capital expenditure by ~1.2% of assets — the median supplier in that sample ($29M market cap) faced interest rates roughly 6x the median buyer's ($23B market cap). This is that study's specific sample, not a general claim about all small-supplier/large-buyer pairs. Murfin & Njoroge, RFS 2015 — Yale Insights summary

Practical tactics + US advocacy

  • Negotiate price and payment terms as separate conversations — bundling them lets the buyer trade a worse term for a price concession without either side noticing the real cost. Source: Phoenix Strategy Group
  • Bring competitive benchmarks into the room — 2–3 alternative-vendor quotes or documented industry-standard terms convert "please pay us faster" into a data-backed ask. Source: Kodiak Hub
  • The US Chamber's Prompt Pay Pledge is the main private-sector lever: 21+ signatories (Intuit, JPMorgan, AGCO, Citadel, and others) commit to faster payment or financing access for small suppliers, plus clear communication of terms. Source: US Chamber of Commerce, Prompt Pay Playbook (PDF)
  • On government contracts specifically, a 2023 Federal Acquisition Regulation rule requires accelerated payments to small-business subcontractors — a rare instance of a legally binding (if narrow) prompt-payment rule in the US. Source: Federal Register, FAR 52.232-40
No US equivalent of a legal maximum term. Unlike the UK (Reporting on Payment Practices, a Small Business Commissioner, and public "late/slow payer" watchlists naming companies like AB InBev at 117 days and Coca-Cola Europacific Partners at 107 days) or the EU's Late Payment Directive, the US has no statutory cap on private B2B payment terms. Prompt-payment progress here runs entirely on voluntary pledges and negotiation leverage — there's no regulator to escalate to.

Sources: Grocery Gazette, Good Business Pays Watchlist (UK context, cited for comparison only).

The existing tools landscape

Almost every well-funded product in this space is a financing tool — it moves money faster around whatever term already got signed. Very few tools help a supplier actually negotiate or benchmark the term itself before signing.

Dedicated tools that help a small supplier benchmark what term it should be getting, or negotiate a shorter one before signing, are scarce — Lucid and generic "contract benchmarking" software are the closest matches found, and neither is built specifically around trade payment terms the way factoring/discounting products are.

Worked example — one invoice, three terms

Illustrative calculation (author's model, not a quoted source): the carrying cost of financing a receivable is invoice × annual cost of capital × (days ÷ 365). Adjust the numbers below — defaults are a $50,000 invoice at a 12% annual cost of capital, a reasonable blended rate for a small business relying on a line of credit or occasional factoring.

Formula: carrying cost = amount × rate × (days ÷ 365). Annualized discount rate = discount% ÷ (100% − discount%) × 365 ÷ days early. Take the discount when its annualized rate is below your cost of capital.
